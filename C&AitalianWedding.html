Benvenuti

Welcome

We could have done this quietly, close to home. But some things deserve a longer table, a slower clock and a view worth travelling for.

So in April 2027 we are taking over a stone villa above Lake Trasimeno — olive terraces, cypresses standing to attention, church bells drifting up from the valley — and asking the people who made us who we are to come and stay a while. Not for an afternoon. For three days.

There will be a dinner cooked in front of you by a chef who won't tell you what's coming next. There will be a ceremony under an Umbrian sky, and a party we fully intend to lose track of. There will be a pizza oven, a swimming pool and one gloriously unserious afternoon to recover in.

We know what we're asking: flights, time off work, a suitcase packed for four days in a country that isn't yours. It is genuinely the biggest thing we could ask of anyone — and the fact that you are reading this means you are the kind of person we would ask.

Everything you need is here: the schedule, how to get to us, where to stay, what to wear, and how to say yes. Start with the Early RSVP — it isn't binding, it just helps us plan — then go and look at the pictures of the lake.

How we got here

Our Story

Camila and Alexander’s story begins in Gainesville, Florida, where they had both arrived for school at the University of Florida. Neither of them came looking for anything in particular. They were there for classes, degrees, and whatever was supposed to come next. Gainesville simply put them in the same place at the same time, and, as it turns out, that was enough.

What followed was a very Florida kind of relationship. Weekends were spent wandering through state parks and nature reserves, learning quickly that alligators should always be given the right of way and that the humidity usually wins. They tried restaurants worth driving across town for and several that definitely were not. They worked their way through breweries, explored whatever was nearby, and sometimes ended the day watching hundreds of thousands of bats pour into the evening sky.

Somewhere between all of that, without either of them noticing exactly when it happened, “getting to know each other” became simply being together.

Not long after they met, Alexander found himself at another wedding: the wedding of Camila’s sister, Susana, and her husband, Alex. Before Alexander was technically part of the family, he was already standing in the family photographs. It also created a small problem that has never really been solved: there were now two Alexes.

Fortunately, Alexander stayed. Otherwise, those family photos would have required a lot more explaining.

Camila is Colombian, Alexander is German-American, and they met in Florida, so their relationship was never going to belong to just one place.

Alexander travelled to Medellín and met Camila’s family. Camila travelled to Germany and met his. There were different traditions, different foods, different rhythms, and sometimes not quite enough shared vocabulary to say everything everyone wanted to say.

But those trips taught them something they have carried with them ever since: people do not always need to speak the same language to become close.

Sometimes it happens simply by being there.

It happens around a table, while someone explains a dish with a combination of words, gestures, facial expressions, and enthusiasm. It happens when someone insists you take another serving, when you learn how a family jokes with one another, what they cook when everyone is together, and which stories get told again and again.

Even when every word is not understood, somehow the important things are.

And little by little, you begin to understand where the person you love comes from.

Alexander has also embraced some parts of Colombian culture more enthusiastically than anyone expected. Most importantly, he now genuinely loves chicharrón, which Camila considers one of the great successes of their relationship.

Over time, their two families have started to feel less like two separate worlds. There are now multiple languages, overlapping traditions, different versions of Christmas, an unreasonable number of group chats, and plenty of things each family has happily borrowed from the other.

What started between two people slowly became something much bigger.

Years later came the proposal. Or, technically, the first almost-proposal.

It was supposed to happen in Hawaii.

Camila was fairly certain something was coming. She had her nails done before the trip, packed particularly good outfits, and generally prepared herself for the possibility that photographs involving a ring might be taken.

There were plenty of photographs.

There was no ring.

In the end, the proposal happened later, at home.

Alexander booked Camila a massage and, once again, an appointment to get her nails done — a detail Camila has since identified as the obvious tell.

Then, in their own living room, with no audience and nothing elaborate to perform, he asked her to marry him.

After all the plans and almost-plans, it turned out to be exactly right.

That evening they went to the Christmas market in Atlanta, wandering between wooden stalls with mulled wine and cold hands, surrounded by a small piece of Germany in the middle of the city.

The proposal in Hawaii would have been beautiful.

The one at home was theirs.

Travel had already become one of the things Camila and Alexander loved most about being together, and Italy had been somewhere on the list for years.

For Alexander, Europe has always felt like home in one direction. For Camila, Italy carried a different kind of meaning.

Long before she met Alexander, Camila had travelled through Italy with her family. While visiting Orvieto, standing among its old streets, cliffs, and cathedral, she remembers having a very specific thought: one day, she wanted to come back there with the person she was going to marry.

At the time, Alexander had not yet entered the story.

Years later, while planning their wedding, Camila and Alexander realized that Orvieto was less than an hour from the place where they had chosen to get married.

They discovered that afterward.

Neither of them is especially good at believing in signs, but they decided that one was worth keeping.

And so the story that began almost accidentally in Gainesville has made its way through Medellín, Germany, Atlanta, Hawaii, countless airports, multiple languages, two families, and more paperwork than anyone would ever include in a traditional love story.

Now it brings them to Italy, to a villa above Lake Trasimeno, and to the people they love most.

Marrying each other feels like the natural part.

Getting friends and family from different cities, countries, cultures, and chapters of their lives onto the same Italian hillside for a few days is the part that feels almost unbelievable.

And maybe that is what this story has always been about: two people meeting in one unexpected place, learning where the other came from, bringing their worlds together, and eventually finding themselves here.

With all of you.

And with, undoubtedly, at least a little chicharrón somewhere in their future.

Getting to us

Travel

Lake Trasimeno sits in the middle of Italy, roughly two hours from Rome and two from Florence. It's easier to reach than it looks — and much easier with a car.

By air · closest

Perugia (PEG)

San Francesco d'Assisi Airport — small, calm, and about 40 minutes' drive from the lake. Seasonal and low-cost routes from several European cities. If you can fly here, fly here.

By air · best connected

Rome & Florence

Rome Fiumicino (FCO) and Florence (FLR) are each roughly a two-hour drive, with the widest choice of long-haul and connecting flights. Rome Ciampino (CIA), Bologna (BLQ) and Pisa (PSA) are also workable at around 2–3 hours.

By rail

Train

The Rome–Florence line runs right past the lake. Aim for Terontola-Cortona or Castiglione del Lago — around 2 hours from Rome Termini and about an hour from Florence S.M.N. Book at trenitalia.com; check timetables closer to the date.

On the ground

Hire a car

Our strong recommendation. The villa and the hill towns are rural, taxis are scarce and expensive, and the driving here is genuinely lovely. Book early for April, and note that most Italian hire cars are manual — request an automatic if you need one.

Paperwork

Before you fly

Check your passport has at least three months' validity beyond April 2027. Non-EU visitors should check whether the EU's new entry requirements apply to them by then. Bring travel insurance, and tell your bank you're travelling.

Weather & packing

April in Umbria

Usually mild and green — pleasant days, properly cool evenings, and a real chance of rain. Pack layers, a jacket for the nights, sunglasses, swimwear for Friday, and shoes that can cope with gravel and grass.

Flying from far away? Our guests are coming from Colombia, Germany and the United States, among others. If you'd like help matching up flights or sharing a hire car with other guests, tell us in your RSVP and we'll happily play matchmaker.

Getting around

Shuttles & Transport

We'll run shuttles

We plan to run coaches between the main guest hotels and the villa for the wedding day, and on the other evenings depending on numbers. It is the safest and simplest way to do this — Umbrian country roads are dark, narrow and not the place for anyone who has enjoyed the wine.

Pick-up points and times will be confirmed once we know where everyone is staying, which is exactly why we ask for two things in the RSVP:

  • The address you're staying at (or the town, if you haven't booked yet)
  • Whether you want a seat, and in which direction

If you'd rather drive

There is parking at the villa and you're very welcome to leave a car overnight and collect it the next day. Italy's drink-drive limit is low (0.5 g/l, and effectively zero for newer drivers) and enforced — please don't risk it.

Taxis

Not something you can hail here. If you need one, book it in advance — every hotel in the area has a number they trust. Uber does not operate here.

Airport transfers

If enough people arrive on similar flights we'll try to group the airport transfers. Send us your flight details in the RSVP once you've booked.

Make a holiday of it

Things To Do

You've come a long way. Stay a few more days — this is one of the loveliest and least crowded corners of Italy, and everything below is within an easy drive.

On the water

Isola Maggiore

A ten-minute ferry from Passignano or Tuoro to a car-free island of fishermen's cottages, lace-makers and one very good lakeside lunch. Half a day, perfectly spent.

Hill towns

Castiglione del Lago & Panicale

Castiglione has a castle on a promontory over the water and a Wednesday market. Panicale is barely more than three squares and a view, and is all the better for it.

Art & pilgrimage

Assisi

About an hour away. The Basilica of San Francesco and the Giotto frescoes are worth the trip whatever you do or don't believe. Go early, before the coaches.

City day

Perugia

Umbria's capital: an Etruscan arch, a great medieval square, a chocolate industry, and a proper aperitivo culture along Corso Vannucci. Park below and take the escalators up.

Just over the border

Cortona, Montepulciano & Pienza

Tuscany starts a few minutes west. Cortona for the views, Montepulciano for the Vino Nobile, Pienza for pecorino and one of the prettiest streets in Italy.

Eat & drink

Oil, wine & a lake fish

Book an olive-oil tasting on the hills above the lake, try the local Trasimeno Gamay, and order the lake fish — regina in porchetta or tegamaccio — at least once.

Outdoors

The lake path

A flat, well-marked cycling and walking route runs around much of the shoreline. Bikes can be hired in Castiglione del Lago and Passignano.

Slow down

Thermal spas

The hot springs at Chianciano Terme and San Casciano dei Bagni are under an hour away, and make an excellent Saturday for anyone in no rush to fly home.

With children

Family days

Boat trips, the castle ramparts at Castiglione, gelato in every town, and the ceramics workshops at Deruta where children can paint their own tile.

The practical bits

Questions

What exactly should I wear?

Wednesday is relaxed — linen, loafers, something warm for the evening. Thursday is the wedding day, and the full dress code comes with the invitation; whatever you choose, avoid stilettos, as much of the day is on grass and gravel. Friday is swimwear, shorts and sunglasses. April evenings here are genuinely cool once the sun goes down, so bring a jacket or a wrap for every night.

Are children invited?

Children are very welcome at the Wednesday dinner and the Friday pool party. We'll confirm the wedding day itself with the formal invitation — either way, tell us names and ages in your RSVP and we'll sort out food and, if there's demand, a babysitter.

Can I bring a plus-one?

Your invitation will name everyone we've been able to include. We're working to a limited number of beds and seats, so if you'd like to ask about a plus-one, please just ask us directly — early, while there's still room to say yes.

Do I need to come for all three days?

Not at all. Come for what works for your time and budget — the wedding day is the one we'd hate you to miss. Just tell us which parts you're joining so we get the numbers right.

What about gifts?

Your presence in Italy is genuinely the gift — the flights alone are more than we'd ever ask for. If you'd still like to give something, we'll share the details closer to the time.

Will there be an unplugged ceremony?

We'll ask you to put your phone away for the ceremony itself and let our photographer do the work. Photograph everything else you like — we'll set up a shared album afterwards so it all ends up in one place.

Practical things: money, phones, language

Italy uses the euro; cards are widely accepted but rural bars and markets still like cash. Tipping is not expected — rounding up is plenty. Most people in the tourist towns speak some English, and any attempt at Italian is met with warmth. EU roaming applies for EU phones; everyone else should check their plan or pick up an eSIM.

What if my plans change?

Tell us as soon as you know, either way. Nobody will be offended — a late "we can't after all" is far easier to absorb than an empty seat, and a late "actually, we can!" is one of our favourite things to hear.
